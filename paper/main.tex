\begin{abstract}
We study exact functional equivalence checking of classical reversible circuits built from
X, CNOT, Toffoli and multi-controlled X gates, by symbolic simulation in the Boolean ring
$\GF[x_1,\dots,x_n]/(x_i^2-x_i)$: every output wire is kept in algebraic normal form (ANF), two
circuits are equivalent iff their output polynomials coincide, and a minimum-degree monomial
of a differing output is a counterexample that we re-check by concrete execution. The
representation, zero-suppressed decision diagrams (ZDDs) over monomial families, is not new:
PolyBoRi uses it for Boolean Gr\"obner bases. Our contribution is engineering and
measurement: \symq{}, an open-source checker with a small native C engine (hash-consed
monomial ZDD, three-product multiplication, lossless operation cache, explicit budgets), and
a protocol that runs every engine under the same driver and controls its caches. Under
identical Python drivers and cold caches, on the 19 supported reversible circuits of the MQT
QCEC test suite, the native engine computes the symbolic outputs \QPBSim$\times$ faster than
PolyBoRi (geometric mean, 95\% CI [\QPBSimLo, \QPBSimHi], \QPBSimWins/\QPBSimN{} circuits;
\QPBSimWarm$\times$ with warm caches) and \QBDDSim$\times$ faster than CUDD BDDs; the
per-call costs of the Python bindings, calibrated separately, do not explain these gaps. On a
blind corpus of 30 generated circuits frozen before any measurement, the gap to PolyBoRi
persists (\BPBSim$\times$ [\BPBSimLo, \BPBSimHi] on the \BPBSimN{} circuits both complete),
the gap to CUDD does not (\BBDDSim$\times$ [\BBDDSimLo, \BBDDSimHi]): CUDD, with dynamic
reordering, completes more circuits and is faster on adders and multipliers.
Sorting the factors of each product by size slows the engine down (\QSortingSim$\times$);
the gate loop fused in C is \QFusedVsLoopSim$\times$ faster than a Python loop over the same
operations, a gain that our first harness hid. All symbolic engines produce identical output
polynomials, also under seven variable orders; the only disagreements, on the blind corpus,
came from our conversion of CUDD BDDs to ANF, which we fixed. Limits: at most 64 variables;
ANF blows up on multipliers under every order we tried; Python drivers; one machine; mostly
sub-millisecond medians. We claim no new algorithm and no general superiority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reversible circuits over X, CNOT (CX), Toffoli (CCX) and multi-controlled X (MCX) gates are
the classical core of many quantum algorithms (arithmetic, oracles,
uncomputation)~\cite{wille2008revlib,gidney2018halving}. Their equivalence is a purely Boolean
question: two circuits on $n$ wires are equivalent iff they compute the same permutation of
$\{0,1\}^n$. Tools from quantum design automation decide it among many other
questions~\cite{burgholzer2021advanced}, and classical techniques (BDDs~\cite{bryant1986graph},
SAT, algebraic methods) apply directly~\cite{wille2009equivalence}.

A particularly simple exact method is symbolic simulation in the Boolean ring: each wire
carries a polynomial over $\GF$ in algebraic normal form (ANF), a gate with controls $C$ and
target $t$ updates $f_t \leftarrow f_t + \prod_{c\in C} f_c$, and canonicity of the ANF turns
equivalence into equality of polynomials (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). The cost is the size of
the intermediate polynomials. Storing them as zero-suppressed decision
diagrams~\cite{minato1993zdd} of their monomial families shares common sub-families, and is
exactly what PolyBoRi does for Boolean Gr\"obner-basis
computations~\cite{brickenstein2009polybori}.

This paper does \emph{not} propose a new representation or a new decision procedure. It asks
an engineering question and tries to answer it without fooling ourselves: \emph{how much does
a small, specialised native engine gain over mature native libraries on this task, once the
driver and the caches are controlled?} Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An exact, budgeted native engine} (Section~\ref{sec:engine}) for monomial ZDDs
  specialised to reversible-circuit simulation, with concretely re-validated counterexamples,
  and its integration in an open-source checker with interchangeable exact backends.
  \item \textbf{A matched-driver, cache-controlled protocol} (Section~\ref{sec:method}) that
  runs every engine under the same minimal Python driver and separates setup, symbolic
  computation, comparison, witness extraction and explicit-monomial extraction, in cold and
  warm cache regimes, with paired statistics and a pre-registered blind corpus.
  \item \textbf{Measurements, including adverse ones} (Section~\ref{sec:results}): the gains
  that survive matching and a blind corpus, the ones that do not (factor sorting; the
  advantage over CUDD), the families where the monomial representation fails, and a
  harness artifact that had hidden the benefit of our fused gate loop.
\end{enumerate}
The phase-sensitive extension of \symq{} to measurement-based uncomputation is a separate,
unfinished line of work and is not evaluated here (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Boolean polynomials and ZDDs.} ZDDs~\cite{minato1993zdd} are reduced, ordered
decision diagrams whose nodes with a $0$ high child are suppressed; they represent families of
sparse sets compactly and canonically~\cite{minato2001zdd}. Minato already used them to
manipulate polynomials implicitly~\cite{minato1995polynomials}. Representing a Boolean
polynomial by the ZDD of its monomial family, with ring operations implemented as recursive
ZDD operations, is the core of PolyBoRi~\cite{brickenstein2009polybori}, maintained today as
BRiAl~\cite{brial} and reachable from Python through a modular SageMath
distribution~\cite{passagemath}; its authors applied it to formal verification of
circuits~\cite{brickenstein2008groebner}. Our engine uses the same representation and the
same kind of recursion; it differs in scope (at most 64 variables, one operation pattern), in
engineering choices (Section~\ref{sec:engine}), and in being budgeted. BDDs over Shannon
cofactors~\cite{bryant1986graph}, as in CUDD~\cite{somenzi2015cudd} (used here through
\texttt{dd}~\cite{dd060}), represent the same Boolean functions without exposing their ANF;
CUDD's ZDD products operate on covers, not on $\GF$ sums, so CUDD offers no ANF ring.

\paragraph{Functional decision diagrams.} The data structure itself is not new. Functional
decision diagrams~\cite{kebschull1992fdd} decompose a function by the positive Davio
expansion $f = f_0 \oplus x\,(f_0 \oplus f_1)$; with the rule that deletes a node whose Davio
child is $0$~\cite{drechsler1996ofdd}, the reduced ordered FDD of $f$ is, node for node, the
ZDD of the monomial family of its ANF under the same order, as Minato
observes~\cite{minato2001zdd} (\texttt{paper/tools/ofdd\_vs\_zdd.py} checks this exhaustively
for every function of at most four variables). Kronecker FDDs mix Shannon and Davio
decompositions per variable~\cite{drechsler1994okfdd}, and the sizes of BDDs and FDDs can
differ exponentially in both directions~\cite{becker1995relation}. What this paper studies is
therefore not the diagram but its use: the $\GF$ product (monomial multiplication with
cancellation modulo $2$, not the unate cube-set product of ZDD packages) driven gate by gate
by reversible circuits, its cost, and how it compares with matched baselines. Positive-polarity
Reed--Muller expansions are also the basis of reversible-logic
synthesis~\cite{agrawal2004synthesis,gupta2006algorithm,maslov2007techniques}, and
ReVerC~\cite{amy2017reverc} compiles reversible circuits with a formally verified compiler.

\paragraph{Algebraic verification of arithmetic circuits.} Computer-algebra verification of
gate-level multipliers fights the monomial blow-up that we also observe, with
backward rewriting and vanishing-monomial removal~\cite{mahzoon2022revsca2}, combinations with
SAT~\cite{kaufmann2023amulet2}, or linear relations extracted from Gr\"obner
bases~\cite{kaufmann2025linear}. Our forward simulation uses none of these techniques and is
not competitive on multipliers; we do not compare with these tools, which target
And-Inverter Graphs rather than reversible circuits.

\paragraph{Equivalence checking of reversible and quantum circuits.} Equivalence of
reversible circuits has been decided with decision diagrams and SAT~\cite{wille2009equivalence};
RevLib~\cite{wille2008revlib} provides benchmarks. For quantum circuits, decision-diagram
checkers go back to QuIDD~\cite{viamontes2007checking}; MQT
QCEC~\cite{burgholzer2021advanced,burgholzer2021qcec,qcecrepo} combines decision-diagram,
simulation and ZX checkers. On X/CX/MCX circuits, unitary equality coincides with functional
equality, so QCEC is a natural point of comparison even though it answers a more general
question and builds no polynomial. Path sums~\cite{amy2019towards} carry, for reversible
gates, exactly our ANF outputs together with a phase polynomial; phase-based methods
optimise and check larger gate sets~\cite{amy2014tpar}. QuPRS combines path-sum reduction with
weighted model counting~\cite{huang2026quprs}, and FeynmanDD reduces path-integral questions
to counting with classical decision diagrams~\cite{wang2025feynmandd}. Closest to our
representation, Irene~\cite{ke2026irene} checks hybrid quantum programs, represents Boolean
expressions as XOR--AND graphs rather than ANF, and reports, counting ANF monomials with ZDDs,
that explicit ANF would need more than 6\,GiB on 30 of its program pairs: independent evidence
that ANF is the wrong representation for some workloads. Measurement and feed-forward are
handled by dedicated checkers~\cite{hong2022dynamic,burgholzer2022nonunitaries,
ricciardi2025hybrid} and by outcome-complete stabilizer
simulation~\cite{kliuchnikov2023stabilizer,kliuchnikov2026phased}; they are outside our
scope.

\paragraph{What is new here.} \texttt{paper/CLAIMS.md} lists each claim with its prior art
and the experiment that could refute it. In short: the representation and the product
recursion are known; a specialised, budgeted native engine and, above all, an evaluation that
controls driver and cache effects are what we contribute. We found no prior evaluation of a
monomial-ZDD engine for reversible-circuit equivalence against PolyBoRi and CUDD under matched
drivers, but our search (arXiv full-text queries and the references above) is not exhaustive.

\section{Problem and semantics}\label{sec:problem}

\begin{definition}[Circuits]
A circuit of width $n$ is a list of gates $(C,t)$ with $C\subseteq\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ and
$t\notin C$. On a state $s\in\{0,1\}^n$ the gate sets $s_t\leftarrow s_t\oplus\prod_{c\in C}s_c$
(X: $C=\emptyset$; CX: $|C|=1$; CCX: $|C|=2$; MCX otherwise).
\end{definition}

Let $B_n=\GF[x_0,\dots,x_{n-1}]/(x_i^2-x_i)$. Each element has a unique ANF, a sum of distinct
square-free monomials, identified with a family of subsets of $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$; addition is
symmetric difference of families.

\begin{fact}[canonicity]
$B_n$ is isomorphic to the ring of Boolean functions $\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$; two polynomials
denote the same function iff their monomial families are equal.
\end{fact}

\emph{Symbolic simulation} starts from $f_i=x_i$ and applies $f_t\leftarrow f_t+\prod_{c\in
C}f_c$ for each gate.

\begin{proposition}[soundness and completeness]
For every input $a$, $(f_0(a),\dots,f_{n-1}(a))$ is the circuit's output on $a$. Hence two
circuits are equivalent iff their output families are equal wire by wire.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Induction on gates: evaluation at $a$ is a ring homomorphism $B_n\to\GF$, so
$(f_t+\prod f_c)(a)=s_t\oplus\prod s_c$; combine with the fact.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[minimum-degree witness]\label{prop:witness}
If $p\neq 0$ and $M$ is a monomial of $p$ of minimum degree, then $p(\chi_M)=1$, where
$\chi_M$ sets exactly the variables of $M$ to 1.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$p(\chi_M)$ is the parity of the number of monomials of $p$ contained in $M$. A monomial
strictly contained in $M$ would have smaller degree, so only $M$ itself counts.
\end{proof}

Applied to $f^L_i+f^R_i$ for the first differing output $i$, Proposition~\ref{prop:witness}
yields an input on which the circuits differ. \symq{} re-executes both circuits on it and
raises an internal-inconsistency error if they agree, so a faulty backend can never return
an unchecked counterexample. Equivalence verdicts have no such independent check; they rest
on the propositions above and on differential testing (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Full
statements, including budgets, are in \texttt{paper/FORMAL\_MODEL.md}; none of them is
machine-checked.

\section{The native engine}\label{sec:engine}

\paragraph{Representation.} Nodes are triples $(\ell,\mathit{lo},\mathit{hi})$ in a single
array, hash-consed through an open-addressing unique table; node $0$ is the empty family
($0$), node $1$ is $\{\emptyset\}$ ($1$). A node at level $\ell$ with variable
$v=x_{\pi(\ell)}$ denotes $\mathit{lo}+v\cdot\mathit{hi}$; zero suppression forbids
$\mathit{hi}=0$; both children lie at strictly larger levels (or are terminals), which is what
makes the representation canonical. Children are always created before their parent, so they have smaller
identifiers, and every bottom-up quantity (monomial count, minimum-degree monomial) is computed
by a single pass in identifier order, without recursion.

\paragraph{Operations.} Addition is the symmetric-difference recursion. Multiplication splits
both operands on the top variable $v$, with $P=P_0+vP_1$ and $Q=Q_0+vQ_1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
PQ = P_0Q_0 + v\,(P_0Q_1 + P_1Q_0 + P_1Q_1),
\end{equation}
which follows from $v^2=v$. Terminal cases include $P\cdot P=P$ (every element of $B_n$ is
idempotent) and $P+P=0$. An algebraically equal two-product form,
$v((P_0+P_1)(Q_0+Q_1)+P_0Q_0)$, built up to two orders of magnitude more nodes on one of our
circuits under a reversed variable order, so we use~\eqref{eq:product}; we make no asymptotic
claim about either form.

\paragraph{Engineering choices.} (i) A lossless operation cache: entries are never evicted
and the table grows, unlike the lossy computed tables of general-purpose packages; managers are
short-lived (one per verification), so no garbage collection is performed. (ii) Monomial
counts are stored per node at creation as signed 64-bit integers. A 64-variable family can
have up to $2^{64}$ monomials, and in the measured version such a count can overflow, which
is undefined behaviour in C (with the compiler used, in practice a wrap-around that no check
detects); we found this defect while preparing this paper. Its fix (saturating counts, and an error
when a saturated polynomial is materialised) is in the repository but \emph{not} in the
measured binary. Counts drive budgets, factor ordering, extraction and the choice of representation in
\symq{}'s adaptive backend, never a verdict (equality is node identity), and
the largest output we measured has \BMaxTerms{} monomials. (iii) Monomials are 64-bit masks, which bounds the width to 64.
(iv) A \texttt{simulate} entry point takes the whole gate list (control indices, offsets,
targets) and runs the loop in C, multiplying the controls of each gate in increasing
monomial count. (v) Budgets on time (checked every 1\,024 recursive steps and every 64
gates), nodes (at creation) and monomials (when a polynomial is materialised) raise an
exception; no partial result is ever returned. The engine is about 1\,240 lines of C against
the CPython API and is optional: without a compiler, the pure-Python backends remain.

\section{Evaluation methodology}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Questions.} (Q1) How fast is the native engine relative to PolyBoRi and CUDD when
only the engine \emph{and its Python binding} differ (the driver being identical)? (Q2) Which parts of the engine and driver matter (ablation)?
(Q3) Does the comparison survive circuits that influenced no design decision? (Q4) Are all
results exact?

\paragraph{Drivers.} Every engine is run under the same drivers: (a) \emph{minimal loop}: the
same Python loop for all tools, which multiplies the controls of each gate in gate order and
adds the product to the target, with no guard and no counter; only the two ring operations
differ (for PolyBoRi, Python's \texttt{*} and \texttt{+} on \texttt{BooleanPolynomial}; for
CUDD through \texttt{dd}~\cite{dd060}, conjunction and \texttt{xor}); (b) \emph{minimal loop,
sorted}: the same with factors sorted in Python by monomial count (\symq{}: its stored
count; PolyBoRi: \texttt{len}); (c) \emph{\symq{}'s \texttt{Algebra} driver}, which decodes
each gate and applies its guards and counters inside the timed phase; (d) for \symq{} only,
the fused C loop that it ships, fed control, offset and target arrays built before the timer
(as the gate list of the loops is). Equality is node identity for \symq{} and CUDD and
\texttt{(a+b).is\_zero()} for PolyBoRi (Sage's \texttt{==} compares term by term and is an
order of magnitude slower on large outputs). The loop calls the engines through their
Python bindings (a C extension for \symq{}, Cython for PolyBoRi and \texttt{dd}), whose
per-call costs differ; we measure them separately (Section~\ref{sec:results}) rather than
attribute the whole loop time to the engine.

\paragraph{Cache regimes.} SageMath caches \texttt{BooleanPolynomialRing} objects by their
arguments: two rings of the same width are the same object, with the same operation cache.
In our earlier harness every PolyBoRi repetition after the first therefore reused one ring
and its caches (an inference from Sage's caching behaviour, which we did not measure in those
runs), while every other engine started from a fresh manager. We now measure two regimes for
every engine: \emph{cold} (a fresh manager per run; for PolyBoRi, a ring with fresh variable
names) and \emph{warm} (one manager reused after a filling run, i.e.\ re-verification).
Setup (manager and variables) is timed separately from the symbolic computation.

\paragraph{Corpora.} (1) The 19 supported circuits of MQT QCEC's
\texttt{test/circuits/original} (16--35 wires; one circuit with controlled-$V$ gates is
rejected), imported byte for byte with hashes and license~\cite{qcecrepo}. They have
influenced our design, so they are not a holdout. (2) A \emph{blind} corpus of 30 circuits
generated by a seeded script and frozen by a hash manifest before any symbolic measurement:
Cuccaro adders~\cite{cuccaro2004adder} and controlled adders up to 31 bits, shift-and-add multipliers up to 10 bits,
incrementers up to 64 bits, and seeded random MCX circuits of 16, 32 and 64 wires. Hypotheses
and analysis were pre-registered; one amendment (cache regimes, timeout censoring, parallel
workers) was recorded before any blind measurement was inspected. Workloads: \emph{simulate}
(symbolic outputs), \emph{equiv} (against a variant built from sound rewrites) and
\emph{nonequiv} (against the circuit with one inserted MCX gate).

\paragraph{Measurement.} One fresh worker process per (circuit, workload, mode), pinned to
one core of a 4-core Intel Xeon VM with about 15\,GiB of memory shared by all processes
(CPython 3.12.3; extension built with GCC 13.3.0 and \texttt{-O3}\footnote{The records of the
blind run list the output of \texttt{cc --version} (clang 18), not the compiler that built
the extension; the measured binary, identified by its SHA-256, carries GCC 13.3.0 in its
\texttt{.comment} section.}). Every run uses two workers on two dedicated cores (isolated re-runs: one); the blind
run shared the machine with an earlier QCEC-corpus run (superseded) and lighter jobs, the
other runs with lighter jobs only. One warmup run, then the median of 7 runs (3 above 1\,s, 1
above 10\,s); 120\,s per cell; cells of one family (random circuits: of one gate and control
count) run in increasing size and larger ones are censored after a timeout. Ratios are paired per
circuit; we report geometric means with 95\% percentile-bootstrap intervals over circuits,
the range and the number of wins. Explicit monomial extraction is timed once and feeds an
output fingerprint compared across all modes. Versions: PolyBoRi through passagemath-brial
10.8.13, CUDD 3.0.0 through \texttt{dd} 0.6.0, MQT QCEC 3.11.0 restricted to its complete
alternating checker (no simulation or ZX checker, so every conclusive verdict is a proof).

\paragraph{Exactness checks.} Beyond the cross-mode fingerprints, the test suite of the
measured commit (2\,490 tests) compares every backend, the native one included, with
concrete execution on all inputs of seeded random circuits of up to 7 wires, checks the
native engine against the Python ZDD node for node, and shows that the same differential
checks catch deliberately broken Python algebras (missing cross term, no cancellation, OR
instead of XOR). Runs of the
native tests under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer were added together with
the overflow fix and are not part of the measured commit. These are tests, not proofs: no
part of the engine is machine-checked.

\paragraph{Exactness (Q4).} A group is one (circuit, workload) cell across all symbolic
modes. For \emph{simulate} the compared fingerprint is a hash of the explicit output monomials
(the polynomials themselves); for \emph{equiv} and \emph{nonequiv} it is only the verdict and
the index of the first differing output, and every counterexample is re-checked concretely. On
the QCEC corpus, \QGroups{} groups were compared with \QDisagree{} disagreement; QCEC returned
\QQCECOk{} correct proofs. \BlindExactness{} No counterexample failed its concrete re-check.
The \BDisagree{} disagreements (\emph{simulate} on \texttt{mult\_6}, \texttt{rand\_16\_100\_2},
\texttt{rand\_16\_100\_4}) all set CUDD with dynamic reordering against every other engine,
CUDD with a static order included. The BDDs were right (on \texttt{rand\_16\_100\_2} they
agree with concrete execution on 300 random inputs); our Reed--Muller conversion of BDDs to ANF, used only for fingerprints,
memoised transforms by node identifier without keeping the nodes alive, so CUDD reused freed
nodes for other functions. A regression test now reproduces this, and the fix keeps the nodes
referenced; timings are unaffected, since conversion is outside the timed phase. All engines
that build ANF agree on every group.

\paragraph{Matched comparison (Q1).} Table~\ref{tab:matched} is the main result. With the same
minimal loop and cold caches, the native engine is \QPBSim$\times$ faster than PolyBoRi on
\emph{simulate} over the QCEC corpus and wins on all \QPBSimN{} circuits; equivalence and
non-equivalence checks, which also simulate a second circuit and compare, show
\QPBEq$\times$ and \QPBNeq$\times$. With warm caches (one manager reused after a filling run)
the ratio drops to \QPBSimWarm$\times$ [\QPBSimWarmLo, \QPBSimWarmHi]: part of the cold-cache
gap is the cost of filling PolyBoRi's caches. Against CUDD BDDs, which represent functions
rather than monomials, the native engine is \QBDDSim$\times$ faster with dynamic reordering
and \QBDDStaticSim$\times$ with a static order. Fixed costs are timed separately: a fresh
PolyBoRi ring costs \SetupPB\,ms and a CUDD manager \SetupBDD\,ms (medians over the QCEC
corpus), against \SetupNative\,ms for the native manager; they dominate end-to-end times on
these small circuits, which is why we do not headline end-to-end ratios.

\paragraph{Binding costs.} The minimal loop calls each engine through its Python binding. We
calibrated one product and one sum of two variables, a cache hit for every engine, in a tight
loop (run \texttt{\BindCommit}): \BindNativeMul/\BindNativeAdd\,ns for the native engine,
\BindPBMul/\BindPBAdd\,ns for PolyBoRi and \BindBDDMul/\BindBDDAdd\,ns for CUDD (product/sum),
against \BindFloor\,ns for an empty Python call. Subtracting these costs, times the number of
calls of each circuit, from both sides is a bound rather than a correction (the calibration
also removes a cache lookup); it leaves ratios of \QBindPBAdjusted$\times$ against PolyBoRi
and \QBindBDDAdjusted$\times$ against CUDD on the QCEC corpus. The bindings take a median
\QBindShareNative\% of the native loop time and \QBindPBShare\% of PolyBoRi's, so they do not
explain the gap; they narrow it.

\paragraph{Ablation (Q2).} Table~\ref{tab:ablation} decomposes the distance between the
Python ZDD and the shipped engine. The C core alone, under \symq{}'s \texttt{Algebra} driver,
gives \QCoreSim$\times$ cold (but only \QCoreSimWarm$\times$ warm, when nearly every operation
is a cache hit and the driver dominates). Replacing that driver by the minimal loop gives
another \QDriverSim$\times$: \symq{}'s per-gate guards and counters are a large share of the
cost of small circuits. Sorting factors by monomial count, which our fused loop does,
\emph{slows} the engine (\QSortingSim$\times$); fusing the loop in C then gains
\QFusionSim$\times$. The shipped fused loop against the unsorted minimal Python loop is
\QFusedVsLoopSim$\times$ (95\% CI [\QFusedVsLoopSimLo, \QFusedVsLoopSimHi],
\QFusedVsLoopSimWins/\QFusedVsLoopSimN{} wins). Our first matched harness found no gain
(0.97$\times$): it built the fused loop's index arrays and gathered statistics inside the
timed phase, while the Python loops received a pre-built gate list. Fed the same pre-built
input, fusion removes about half of the time of these small circuits, consistent with the
binding costs above; sorting is the part that does not pay.

\paragraph{Variable order (robustness).} Table~\ref{tab:order} varies the variable order of
the native engine (runs \texttt{\QOrderCommit}; identity, reversed and five seeded random

\section{Threats to validity and limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single machine, small circuits.} Medians are often below one millisecond; one
  VM; no CPU frequency control. Paired ratios and bootstrap intervals over circuits do not
  remove machine effects.
  \item \textbf{Drivers and bindings.} The minimal loop is Python for every engine, and the
  bindings differ in per-call cost. We calibrate that cost and bound its effect
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}), but a native driver for PolyBoRi (its C++ API) or CUDD (its C
  API) on the same gate arrays is the only clean answer and is future work.
  \item \textbf{Memory and caches.} The native engine never evicts its operation cache and
  never collects garbage; CUDD runs with garbage collection and \texttt{dd}'s default cache
  limits; PolyBoRi with its defaults. We report no memory comparison (the recorded peak-RSS
  growth is not comparable across tools), and in the blind corpus a native cell can die from
  memory exhaustion rather than time out. Part of the cold/warm difference may be page
  faults and lazy initialisation inside the timed phase rather than cache contents.
  \item \textbf{Missing baselines.} We compare with no SAT-based combinational equivalence
  checker (e.g.\ ABC's \texttt{cec}~\cite{brayton2010abc}, which uses SAT sweeping~\cite{mishchenko2006cec}, on
  a miter) and no path-sum
  verifier (Feynman~\cite{amy2019towards}); both are natural answers to the cases where ANF
  explodes.
  \item \textbf{Configuration.} PolyBoRi uses the \texttt{lp} order; CUDD its defaults, with and
  without dynamic reordering; no tuned variable order for anyone.
  \item \textbf{Representation and order.} Width at most 64; monomial representations
  explode on multipliers (Tables~\ref{tab:status} and~\ref{tab:order}); the default
  variable order fails on adders that other orders handle, and we have no ordering
  heuristic; no garbage collection, so memory grows with the
  number of operations of a verification.
  \item \textbf{Corpus.} The QCEC circuits influenced the design; the blind corpus is
  generated, not gathered from independent sources. Many QCEC test circuits come from RevLib;
  we rely on the licence of the QCEC repository and have not traced each file's provenance.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} Classical reversible gates only. \symq{} also contains a specification
  and reference implementation of a restricted phase fragment ($\pm1$ phases, X-basis
  measure-and-reset, single-outcome Z feed-forward) aimed at measurement-based
  uncomputation~\cite{gidney2018halving}; it has proof sketches and exhaustive small-case
  tests against an independent state-vector oracle, but no external corpus and no budgeted
  backend, and is not a contribution of this paper. Outcome-complete phase simulation and
  hybrid-circuit checkers~\cite{kliuchnikov2026phased,ricciardi2025hybrid,ke2026irene}
  address related, broader questions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A specialised native monomial-ZDD engine checks the equivalence of small reversible circuits
several times faster than PolyBoRi and CUDD when every tool is driven identically and caches
are controlled; most of what looked like the benefit of a fused C loop was the removal of our
own driver overhead, and sorting factors by size hurts. These are engineering results on one
machine and two small corpora, not a claim of algorithmic novelty or general superiority.
